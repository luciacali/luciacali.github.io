## The Bigger Picture

Let's take a step back for a moment, and consider the major advancements -- and when the happened -- that now support these technologies. 

Beginning in 1990, the Human Genome Project took thousands of scientists, across 6 countries, over 13 years and $3 billion to map the human genome. Today, with next-generation sequencing technology, it takes one technician around 48 hours and less than 100 bucks to do the same thing, but better (92% at the culmination of the HGP in 2003, opposed to 100%, or telemere-to-telemere coverage today). This technology emerged in 2011. Only *eight years* after the multi-national, multi-billion dollar "old-gen" sequencing project concluded.

Occurring at the same exact time, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuel Charpentier were racing to get their work on CRISPR-Cas9 (the genome editing tool) into print. 

To put this into perspective, say you have your first baby in 2003, and your second in 2012. Just as your first child is born, the midical community finally has a tangible picture of how that child's (and all other peoples) genome is structured. By the time the child is eight, our knowledge of human genetics grows marginally, but not at the rate we had hoped upon mapping the human genome. Say you go on parental leave in early 2011, and return to work when the child is two years old. By the time you return our science allows us to map *fully* the genome of your specific baby, and the technology (if we are to be so ignorant) to *alter* their heritable traits. Not a radical fantasy, but a terrible reality; read about He Jiankui 2018 below.
