**DOI:**

## Scope of the Paper

&emsp;In the correspondence above, the authors impress upon the reader the dangers of deep learning when paired with biotechnology. As both fields are essentially experiencing explosive advancement simultaneously, there is substantial grey area surrounding regulation, control, and security as these technologies begin to merge.

&emsp;This blend holds promise for advancements in drug development, gene therapies, imaging, research, and other medical uses. However, just as harnessing the power of the atom can lead to near limitless energy, there is conversely the potential for weapons of mass destruction. 

&emsp;The authors of this paper warn against AI being used to assist in work which can threaten national biosecurity. Among them include bypassing DNA synthesis firewalls, and to reproduce &mdash; wholly or in part &mdash; the genes which make viruses harmful and virulent (able to spread). 
  
## What I think

&emsp;Realistically, the danger of AI in biotechnology is not an AI agent turned Terminator that seeks to destroy all humans. It is individuals with malicious intent that now have access to a technology that is as powerful as 100 experts working together. AI has done something that no other technology has ever been able to produce; autonomous knowledge compression. For all of human history, information gathered from multiple sources was articulated by a human mind and filtered through all of the individuals opinions, morals, and biases. Although for thousands of years our technology to record and obtain information has advanced (written language, to the printing press, to the internet), AI offers for the first time something new and powerful. The ability to gather information from an enormous number of sources, and synthesize it into a conversational interface.

&emsp;This should be respected as nothing short of a miracle, and many people are eagerly adopting AI into businesses, education, and research. However, jail-broken models allow users to bypass the opinions, morals, and biases that a human mind is inarguably entitled to. That is where the real danger lies. 

&emsp;A human might refuse to help someone when they realize a dangerous application, an institution may refuse access, a synthesis company may screen and flag an order, and universities may have biosecurity oversight. Sure, dangerous technologies may still be produced, but there is *friction* built into every step of the process. An AI model's constraints rely on the technical and institutional safeguards surrounding its deployment, and those safeguards can be deliberately weakened or bypassed. In other words, we can order it to provide no friction.

## The Bigger Picture

Let's take a step back for a moment, and consider the major advancements &mdash; and when they happened &mdash; that now support these technologies. 

&emsp;Beginning in 1990, the Human Genome Project required thousands of scientists, across 6 countries, over 13 years and 3 billion dollars to map the human genome. Today, with next-generation sequencing technology, a similarly enormous amount of genomic data can be obtained in days for a fraction of the labor the cost. This technology emerged around 2011. Only *eight years* after the multi-national, multi-billion dollar "old-gen" sequencing project concluded.

Occurring at the same exact time that next-generation sequencing was beginning to emerge, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuel Charpentier were racing to get their work on CRISPR-Cas9 (the gene editing tool) into print. 

&emsp;To put this into the perspective of a human lifetime, say you have your first baby in 2003, and your second in 2012. Just as your first child is born, the medical community finally has a tangible picture of how that child's (and all other peoples) genome is structured. By the time the child is eight, our knowledge of human genetics grows marginally, but not at the rate which we had hoped for upon mapping the human genome. Say you go on parental leave in early 2011, and return to work when the child is two years old. By the time you return, our science allows us to map the genome of *your specific baby*, and the technological capacity (if we are to be so ignorant) to *alter* their heritable traits. Not a radical fantasy, but a terrible reality; read about He Jiankui 2018 below.

&emsp;These striking advancements have already launched biological research into orbit this century. Small academic labs can now perform research that was unthinkable in the late 20th century, and we have seen many products of this technological leap that are hugely beneficial to us (targeted oncology, epidemiological surveillance, and mRNA vaccines to name a few). There is no denying that fast, affordable, high-throughput technology has benefited the medical community and society as a whole. The rapid advancements we have seen so far this century are largely due to the democratization of capability. Small laboratories and independent researchers can now contribute to discoveries that were until recently, restricted to elite institutions. The problem is that democracy does not discriminate between beneficial and harmful applications.

&emsp;Perhaps the most imperative security difference over the last 100 years is not technological accessibility, but governance. With the Manhattan Project there were centralized facilities, military oversight, compartmentalization, security procedures, and multiple officials involved in major decisions. None of these things made the project morally uncomplicated, but again, they created friction, and friction matters enormously in national security.

&emsp;Think of all the voices, procedures, security clearances, and restrictions that are involved with weapons manufacture. These checkpoints along the process let multiple people at multiple levels hit the brakes and ask, "When, where, how, and why are we going to use this?" As projects continue to require less specialized instruments, less people, less funding, and perhaps an AI assistant, friction begins to disappear *rapidly.* All of a sudden it becomes much more difficult to control the development and manufacture of dangerous products.

## Alright, Wrap it Up

&emsp;Perhaps the most compelling message from Wang et al. is the call for checks and balances in biotech that are tailored for AI-driven discovery and development. For better or worse, the AI genie is out of the bottle. The most important thing to focus on by the end of this decade is to decide where to place security safeguards, and how to regulate AI-driven biological research occurring withing the research labs across the country and world. Is it by releasing constrained AI models? Is it by regulating routine auditing of laboratories? Perhaps we should require background checks and security clearances for scientists? Whatever it is, we have hundreds of years of media warning us about the dangers of artificial intelligence and their unintended consequences. How then, can we embrace Frankenstein's creation in a constructive manner, and how do we prevent it from becoming "the Monster?"
