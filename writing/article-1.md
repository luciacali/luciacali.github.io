## Scope of the Paper

&emsp;In the correspondence above, the authors impress upon the reader the dangers of deep learning when paired with biotechnology. As both fields are essentially experiencing explosive advancement simultaneously, there is lot of grey area surrounding regulation, control, and security as these technologies merge.

&emsp;While this tech holds promise for advancing drug development, gene therapies, imaging, research and other medical uses, powerful tools will always double as deadly weapons by those who abuse them. Just as harnessing the power of the atom can lead to near limitless energy, there is conversely the potential for weapons of mass destruction. 

&emsp;The authors of this paper warn against AI being able to bypass DNA synthesis firewalls, to reproduce &mdash; wholly or in part &mdash; the genes which make viruses harmful and virulent (able to spread).

## The Bigger Picture

Let's take a step back for a moment, and consider the major advancements &mdash; and when they happened &mdash; that now support these technologies. 

&emsp;Beginning in 1990, the Human Genome Project (HGP) required thousands of scientists, across 6 countries, over 13 years and $3 billion to map the human genome. Today, with next-generation sequencing technology, it takes one technician around 48 hours and less than 100 bucks to do the same thing, only better (92% at the culmination of the HGP in 2003, opposed to 100%, or telemere-to-telemere coverage today). This technology emerged in 2011. Only *eight years* after the multi-national, multi-billion dollar "old-gen" sequencing project concluded.

Occurring at the same exact time, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuel Charpentier were racing to get their work on CRISPR-Cas9 (the gene editing tool) into print. 

&emsp;To put this into the perspective of a human lifetime, say you have your first baby in 2003, and your second in 2012. Just as your first child is born, the medical community finally has a tangible picture of how that child's (and all other peoples) genome is structured. By the time the child is eight, our knowledge of human genetics grows marginally, but not at the rate which we had hoped for upon mapping the human genome. Say you go on parental leave in early 2011, and return to work when the child is two years old. By the time you return, our science allows us to map the genome of *your specific baby*, and the technological capacity (if we are to be so ignorant) to *alter* their heritable traits. Not a radical fantasy, but a terrible reality; read about He Jiankui 2018 below.
